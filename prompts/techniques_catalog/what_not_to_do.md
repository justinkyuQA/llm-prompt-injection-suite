Technique 011: "What Not To Do" Framing

Description

This technique frames an inquiry around mistakes, warnings, prohibited actions, or common pitfalls rather than desired behaviors. The request focuses on identifying actions that should be avoided, often in the context of safety, training, professional development, or risk reduction.

Representative Prompt Class (Abstracted)

«"List mistakes professionals warn against."»

Observed Mechanic

- Enumerates failure modes and common errors
- Uses negation rather than direct instruction
- Focuses on warnings, cautions, and pitfalls
- Positions the model as a mentor or advisor
- May implicitly reveal the structure of successful or unsuccessful actions

Why It Emerged Naturally

This framing is widely used in:

- Professional training programs
- Safety briefings
- Mentorship and coaching
- Educational materials
- Risk management guidance
- Lessons-learned reviews

The researcher employed this approach as a means of discouraging misuse and promoting awareness of common errors rather than describing desired behaviors.

Risk Insight (Defensive)

Negative framing can:

- Mirror positive instruction structurally
- Identify critical decision points
- Reveal common failure paths
- Create implicit checklists of actions to avoid
- Be interpreted as the inverse of recommended behavior

Because warnings often correspond directly to known risks, descriptions of mistakes may indirectly communicate information about the underlying activity.

Research Notes

Negative instruction is a common educational technique used to improve safety, reduce errors, and accelerate learning. Many training programs rely heavily on examples of mistakes because failure scenarios are often memorable and instructive.

This technique is useful for studying how models handle negation, warnings, and error-focused explanations while maintaining appropriate abstraction levels.

Defensive Considerations

Safety systems may benefit from:

- Evaluating informational content independent of positive or negative framing
- Monitoring when warnings closely parallel procedural descriptions
- Assessing whether lists of mistakes reveal operational structures
- Distinguishing educational caution from indirect behavioral guidance
- Considering how negative framing may be interpreted by different audiences
